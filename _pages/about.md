I'm a computational neuroscientist with a background in physics and mathematics. My work moves between three things: the theory of how network structure constrains dynamics, models of neural circuits, and the software that turns large neural datasets into results.

My PhD, in Cognitive and Neural Systems at Boston University, was on coupled-cell networks: when cells in a network are able to synchronize, and when they actually do. Since then I have modeled transient beta events in thalamocortical circuits at Brown University, built methods for phenotyping large sleep EEG datasets at the Broad Institute and Harvard Medical School, and, most recently, maintained a two-photon imaging pipeline and developed graph neural networks for connectomics proofreading at Baylor College of Medicine.

The thread through all of it is symmetry. Network symmetries and their generalizations, such as graph fibrations and equitable partitions, determine which patterns of synchrony a system can support, and I think that structure has more to say about biological computation and perception than it is usually asked to.

I'm interested in research scientist and scientific software roles in computational neuroscience, computational biology and AI for science, and in teaching. I'm based in Central Massachusetts and work well remotely.

## Research

**Synchrony and structure in coupled-cell networks.** Stewart's lattice of balanced polydiagonals describes where synchrony is possible in a network of coupled cells. In my thesis I constructed a dual lattice of subspaces transverse to it, proved the two are anti-isomorphic, and showed that contraction respects the ordering. The dual lattice is where synchronization actually happens. When a synchrony subspace and a transverse subspace contract together, a subpopulation of cells is pinned to fixed potentials, a silent configuration I called a glyph. A V1–LGN model built on this gives one account of an unexplained SSVEP effect, where flicker frequencies 1 Hz apart produce alpha-band power differing by an order of magnitude.

**Proofreading connectomes with graph neural networks.** Large electron-microscopy reconstructions such as MICrONS (mouse visual cortex) and H01 (human temporal cortex) contain merge and split errors that are expensive to fix by hand. At Baylor I developed geometric graph neural networks that detect these errors on top of the existing segmentations, and built a pipeline for training and comparing very different models on the same data. Most of the work was in preprocessing and dataset construction, not the model.

## Short bio

Robert Law is a computational neuroscientist whose work spans dynamical systems theory, biophysical modeling and large-scale neural data analysis. He received a PhD in Cognitive and Neural Systems from Boston University and a BS in Physics from Harvey Mudd College. He has held research positions at Brown University, the Broad Institute, Harvard Medical School and Baylor College of Medicine, and has consulted for Cognito Therapeutics and the Levin Lab at Tufts University. His publications cover beta rhythms and tactile perception, sleep neurophysiology in schizophrenia, bioelectric memory, neuromodulator dynamics and voltage imaging.
